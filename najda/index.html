No police operations room receives anything from this page, no car is linked to it, and no message is sent to anybody. Every vehicle, incident, officer and contact on it is invented, and the page makes no network requests at all beyond loading its fonts. It is the design for a service that has not been built. The one real thing here is 112 — Kuwait’s emergency number, which you should call yourself if you are reading this after a crash.

Automatic crash alert · car + phone

Nobody calls for help from inside a bad crash.

Najda runs in two places at once — in the car, on its own crash bus, and in the phone in your pocket. It reads both, decides within a second whether what just happened was a crash or a pothole, and then asks you whether you are alright. If you answer, it stops there. If you don’t, it sends your position, your vehicle and your medical card to the 112 operations room and a plain-language message to the person you chose. This page is the design for that service, not the service. Nothing on it reaches anybody.

The demo clock runs at real speed, so a 30-second window really does take 30 seconds. Nothing here is recorded.

The whole service in four moves

Sense, decide, wait, dispatch.

01Sense

Eight channels at 100 samples a second: three axes of acceleration, rotation, wheel speed, the restraint line, occupant movement and cabin sound.

02Decide

A crash is a shape, not a number. Speed lost in milliseconds, direction of the blow, whether the car stopped and whether the restraints fired — together, not one at a time.

03Wait

Thirty seconds to say you’re fine. Ten if the airbag fired. Nothing at all if the car rolled and nobody has moved since.

04Dispatch

Two different messages: everything the operations room needs, and only what your contact needs. Delivered over whichever radio is still alive.

What it refuses to pretend

Four things this design will not claim.

It never says whether you are hurt.

It knows what happened to the car. It does not know what happened to you, and a system that guesses at injuries over the radio makes the operations room trust it less, not more. Severity here describes the impact and nothing else.

It asks before it calls.

Every dispatch is preceded by a window you can close with one press. The only time it skips the question is when asking is pointless: restraints fired, the car is still, and nobody inside has moved for ten seconds.

One device is not enough.

A phone on its own cannot tell a crash from a phone thrown into a footwell. Phone-only mode still works, waits longer, and says on screen that its confidence is capped.

It is silent on an ordinary drive.

No position, no speed and no audio leaves the car unless a crash has been confirmed and the window has closed. The rest of the time the data lives and dies inside the vehicle.

Two devices, one judgement

The car knows what it hit. The phone knows what happened to you.

Neither one is enough on its own. The car’s bus reports the impact and the restraints but has no idea whether anyone moved afterwards; the phone feels every bump in your pocket and cannot tell a crash from a fall. Najda is the argument between the two. Unlink either below and watch what the rest of the page does about it.

The car linked

Reached over the diagnostic port or the manufacturer’s own telematics unit. In a finished build this is where a factory eCall module would sit.

  • accel.xyzThree axes at 100 Hz, from the car’s own crash sensors rather than a pocket.
  • gyro.zYaw and roll rate — the channel that separates a spin from a roll.
  • wheel.speedGround truth for speed, independent of satellites.
  • restraintAirbag squib and belt pretensioner lines. The single most reliable bit in the system.
  • fuel.pumpCut-off state, which tells the operations room whether fuel is still being pumped.
  • doorsWhether a door has opened since the impact — somebody got out.
Full confidence available.
The phone linked

The part that survives the car losing power, and the only part that can hear the cabin or notice that you have stopped moving.

  • imuAccelerometer and gyroscope, used to confirm the car’s reading, never alone if the car is there.
  • gnssPosition, heading and the road it was matched to.
  • occupantMovement after the impact. Ten seconds of complete stillness is what removes the question.
  • cabin.audioA single acoustic feature — the broadband transient of deforming metal and glass. No recording is kept or sent.
  • radioMobile signal and battery, so the delivery chain knows what it still has.
  • voiceThe speaker and microphone the operations room uses to talk to the cabin once a patrol is assigned.

Why two thresholds and not one. A pothole at speed can put 6 g through a phone in a door pocket while the car loses nothing at all; a 30 km/h wall is 12 g and takes the speed with it. The pair — how hard, and how much speed went with it — is what separates them, and the restraint line settles whatever is left. Every rejection below is a channel disagreeing, and the readout names it.

The window

The part of the design that decides whether anyone trusts it.

A system that calls the police every time you hit a kerb gets switched off within a week, and then it is not there on the day it matters. So every dispatch goes through a window you can close with one press — and the window gets shorter as the evidence gets harder.

The packet

Two messages, because an operations room and your mother need different things.

The operations room gets everything that helps a patrol and an ambulance arrive prepared. Your contact gets where you are, that help is already coming, and nothing about your medical record. This is what left the car in the last dispatch — or what would leave it, if you staged one.

Delivery

Tried in order, and it stops at the first one that gets an acknowledgement. The chain matters more than the packet: the crashes that need this most are the ones that break the aerial.

    What is deliberately not sent
    • audioNo recording of the cabin. One number — whether an impact transient was heard — and it is discarded after the decision.
    • historyNo route, no previous trips, no speeding record. The operations room gets the last 20 seconds and nothing before it.
    • medical → contactBlood type, allergies and medication go to the operations room only. Your contact is told where you are, not what you take.
    • anything, normallyOn a drive with no crash, the answer to every one of these is: nothing leaves the car.
    The other end of the radio

    An operations room does not want an app. It wants a line in a queue.

    This is the supply side: what a dispatcher would see if Najda were allowed to write into the 112 queue. Sorted worst-first, one line each, the whole incident beside it. Acknowledging one writes back into the driver’s own log — the phone in the car says a patrol has been assigned in the same second.

    No such link exists. Writing into a national emergency queue is a legal arrangement before it is a technical one, and it is the part of this product that would take the longest. The console is drawn here to be honest about what the service is asking for, not to suggest it has been granted.

    Who is told, and in what order

    Three people, ranked, and a card only a paramedic sees.

    Contacts are tried in order until one of them acknowledges, then the rest get the same message marked already answered so nobody drives to the scene twice. All of these are invented.

    Medical card

    Goes to the operations room with the dispatch and to nobody else. Four fields, because a field that is usually blank is a field a paramedic stops reading.

    In a finished service this is entered once, encrypted on the phone, and released only by a confirmed dispatch — not readable by the app on an ordinary day.

    The rules around them
    • orderTried one at a time, twelve seconds apart, not all at once — three phones ringing produces three people who each assume the other is going.
    • contentWhere, when, how bad, and that 112 already has it. Never a guess about injuries.
    • minorA minor collision reaches the contacts and stops there. The operations room is not told about a car park.
    • cancelIf you close the window, no contact is ever told an event happened.
    • afterPosition updates stop when a patrol is assigned. The contact gets a scene, not a live location on you.
    Honesty

    Where this design fails, written down before anybody ships it.

    Every one of these is a real failure mode of automatic crash alerting, and none of them is solved by the page you are reading. They are listed because a safety product that only describes its good day is not a safety product.

    It cannot tell whether you are injured.

    Two identical impacts, two very different people. The system reports what it measured and leaves the medicine to the people who arrive.

    A phone in a footwell looks like a crash.

    Which is why phone-only mode caps confidence at 79%, waits longer, and will not dispatch a minor event at all. It is a fallback, not the product.

    No signal, no alert.

    Under a tunnel or out in the desert the chain runs out. The design keeps trying and sends the moment the radio returns, with the original timestamp attached — late help is still help, and a false timestamp is worse than a late one.

    The phone can be destroyed.

    That is the argument for the car’s own modem carrying the first attempt: the part bolted to the vehicle usually survives the part resting on the seat.

    A stationary car with a dead battery says nothing.

    If the crash takes the electrical system and the phone is off, there is no service. No design on this page changes that.

    Somebody has to answer at the other end.

    Without a real agreement with the Ministry of the Interior this is an app that calls your mother. That is worth something, and it is not what it claims to be on the front page.

    Status of this page

    Najda is a prototype. Every vehicle, incident, contact, officer and coordinate on it is invented. No car is linked, no operations room is connected, no message has ever been sent from it, and it makes no network requests beyond loading its fonts. It keeps nothing after you close the tab.

    If you are in a real emergency in Kuwait, call 112. That number is the only thing on this page that reaches anybody.
